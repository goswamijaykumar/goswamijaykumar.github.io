Two-Stage Telescopic ASRS.

An Automated Storage and Retrieval System built to industry standard. I owned the mechanical architecture, centred on a two-stage telescopic mast driven by a single motor through a rack and pinion, with 2:1 chain reeving doubling the carriage travel.

The brief.

Build an Automated Storage and Retrieval System to industry standard, 360 kg of total rack capacity handled roughly 10 kg at a time: store a tote, retrieve it, index the rack, and do it repeatably. I owned the mechanical architecture from concept through analysis and production handoff. The interesting problem was not the frame or the controls. It was the lift.

// owned: mechanical architecture · lift mechanism · FEA · tolerance analysis · DFM

The lifting mechanism.

An ASRS lift has a constraint that makes it more interesting than it first appears: the mast has to reach the top of the rack, but it also has to fit under the ceiling when collapsed. A single-stage mast tall enough to reach the top shelf is too tall to park. That constraint is what forces a telescoping design.

01 · Two-stage telescopic mast

I designed the lift as a two-stage telescopic mast: a fixed outer column, a moving inner stage that rises out of it, and a carriage that rises out of the inner stage. Collapsed, the whole thing is roughly half its extended height. The stages ride on linear guides so the carriage stays square to the rack under an offset load, which matters because a tote sitting out on the forks is a cantilever, not a centred weight.

02 · One motor, rack and pinion

The drive is a linear geared rail: a rack running up the fixed column with a pinion on the moving stage, driven by a single motor. Rack and pinion gives positive engagement with no slip, which is what you want for position repeatability on a rack that has to index to a specific shelf. It also holds position against gravity better than a friction drive.

03 · Chain reeving for the second stage

Driving stage one is straightforward. The question is how the carriage gets up the inner stage without a second motor, and the answer is a chain.

A sprocket sits at the top of the moving inner stage. A chain passes over it with one end anchored to the fixed frame and the other to the carriage. When the pinion drives the inner stage up by some distance, the sprocket travels with it, and because one end of the chain is tied to ground, the chain pays the carriage up by that same distance relative to the stage that is already moving. The carriage therefore rises twice as far as the inner stage, from one motor, with both stages moving together throughout.

This is the same 2:1 reeving used in a forklift duplex mast, where a hydraulic cylinder drives the inner mast and the chain doubles the carriage travel. Here the cylinder is replaced by the rack and pinion.

04 · Chain, not belt

The carriage lifts roughly 10 kg per tote, plus its own mass, hanging off the chain for the entire lift. A toothed belt would have worked on load alone, but chain won on the things that matter over a duty cycle:

  • Strength and margin. Chain takes the working load with far more headroom, and the failure mode is progressive wear rather than a sudden tooth shear.
  • Creep under sustained load. A belt holding a suspended load stretches over time. A chain does not, so the carriage stays where the controller thinks it is.
  • Positive engagement. Chain and sprocket cannot skip a tooth under shock, which is what a belt does exactly once before you stop trusting your position feedback.

Analysis & production handoff.

The design had to survive being manufactured, not just being modelled.

  • FEA on the structural members and the carriage, checking deflection under an offset load rather than only stress at the fixings.
  • Tolerance analysis across the telescoping stages. This is where a telescopic mast is won or lost: stack up the guide clearances wrong and the mast either binds or rattles, and the fix is expensive once parts exist.
  • DFM optimization for production handoff, working the design toward standard sections, available stock, and parts a shop can actually make.

Controls, briefly.

I also built the control and monitoring layer: PLC and HMI logic in CodeSYS, an OPC UA pipeline through a gateway and I/O-link master, and Thingworx with Kepserver for IIoT reporting, so the system publishes its state to a smart manufacturing floor. The full store, retrieve and index cycle runs through it.

Status.

Design complete and handed off; the build moved into manufacturing.
