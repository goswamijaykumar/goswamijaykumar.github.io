14 DoF Bi-Pedal Robot.

A 14-degree-of-freedom biped built to reproduce human walking patterns. I designed the custom PCB that drives it, selected and integrated the encoder-equipped DC motors at every joint, and laid out the wiring harness for a machine that never stops moving.

The brief.

A 14-degree-of-freedom biped built to reproduce human walking patterns. I owned the electronics: the custom PCB that drives it, the motor and encoder selection across every joint, and the wiring that has to survive a machine that moves.

The mechanical structure was someone else's work. Mine was everything that makes fourteen joints actually controllable from one board.

// owned: PCB design · motor & encoder selection · harness layout · bring-up

Driving fourteen joints.

I designed a custom PCB for the robot and took it through the full cycle: schematic capture, board layout, fabrication, and physical bring-up. Bring-up is the part that teaches you things. A board that is correct on paper still has to be probed, powered in stages, and argued with before it drives anything.

I selected and integrated DC motors with encoders at each joint. Fourteen joints means fourteen motor drivers and fourteen encoder channels, and the board has to supply current to all of them without the switching noise from the motor side corrupting the encoder counts on the signal side.

  • Full design cycle owned end to end, schematic through bring-up.
  • Motor and encoder selection matched to the torque and resolution each joint needed.
  • Power and signal separation so encoder feedback stays trustworthy under load.

Wiring a machine that moves.

I designed the wire routing and harness layout, which on a walking robot is a real constraint rather than an afterthought. Every cable crossing a joint flexes each time that joint moves, and a biped does that continuously. Route it badly and the failure is not immediate, it is a conductor that work-hardens and opens up three weeks later during a demo.

  • Harness routed so cables cross joints at low-flex paths rather than the outside of the bend.
  • Strain relief at every connector, because the connector is where a moving harness fails first.
  • Runs bundled and constrained so nothing snags on the structure through the gait cycle.

Distributed control over I²C.

Fourteen joints are more than one controller handles comfortably, so control is distributed across multiple microcontrollers talking over I²C. That keeps the wiring to two shared lines instead of a dedicated bus per controller, at the cost of having to be careful about addressing, pull-ups, and bus length as the robot grew.
